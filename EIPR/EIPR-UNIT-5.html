Industrial Designs · Copyright · Cyber Law · IT Act 2000 · Complete Study Guide with Case Studies

What is an Industrial Design?

An industrial design refers to the ornamental or aesthetic aspect of an article that is generally mass-produced and useful. From an IP law perspective, it covers only the appearance of a product — not its technical or functional aspects.

3D Features
  • Shape of the product
  • Surface treatment
  • Form and contour
2D Features
  • Patterns and ornamentation
  • Lines and colours
  • Combination of above

Key Rule: If a design cannot be applied to an article → it falls under copyright as an artistic work. Some designs may also qualify as trademarks.

Governed by: Designs Act, 2000 (replaced the Designs Act, 1911). Object: reward creators with exclusive use while removing impediments to free use of available designs.

Creative Designs in Business
1. Customise Products

Modify designs for specific age groups, cultures or segments (e.g., children vs. adults for watches). Same function, different design.

2. Create Niche Markets

Differentiate products from competitors through creative design — from ordinary locks to luxury cars.

3. Strengthen Brands

Combine design with trademarks to create or redefine brand identity. Many companies have redefined themselves through product design focus.

Why Protect Industrial Designs?
  • Prevent copying: Registration creates exclusive rights, stopping competitors from imitating your design
  • Return on investment: Ensures fair reward for design and marketing costs
  • Business asset: Registered designs increase company and product commercial value
  • Licensing revenue: Can license or sell to others to enter markets otherwise unreachable
  • Fair competition: Encourages honest trade practices and a diverse range of attractive products
10-Step Procedure for Design Protection
1
Research & Preliminary Assessment

Search existing designs in patent office databases to confirm your design is unique and not already protected.

2
Identify the Jurisdiction

Choose where to file — single country, regional office (EU IPO), or international application via the Hague System (WIPO).

3
Prepare Application Documents

Drawings/illustrations of the design, description of features and functionality, jurisdiction-specific forms.

4
File the Application

Submit to the relevant IP office with all documents and required filing fees.

5
Examination

The IP office assesses whether the design meets legal requirements including novelty and originality.

6
Publication

Accepted applications published in a public database or journal, notifying third parties and opening the opposition window.

7
Opposition Period

Third parties may file objections. If no valid opposition or resolved in your favour, proceeds to registration.

8
Registration

Certificate of registration issued — serves as legal evidence of your design rights.

9
Maintenance & Renewal

Design protection is time-limited. Track renewal deadlines and pay fees to maintain protection.

10
Enforcement

If infringed: cease-and-desist letters, licensing negotiation, or litigation in court.

Grounds for Revocation
  • Lack of novelty or originality

Available Remedies

  • Court injunctions

What is Copyright?

Copyright is the legal right given to creators of literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works to control copying and use of their work. Protection is automatic from the moment of creation — no registration needed.

Important: Mere ideas are NOT protected. Only the expression of ideas is protected. Confirmed by TRIPS Agreement Article 9.2.

Economic Rights
  • Reproduction
  • Broadcasting
  • Public performance
  • Adaptation & translation
  • Public recitation & display
  • Distribution
Moral Rights
  • Right to claim authorship
  • Right to object to distortion, mutilation or modification harmful to reputation
  • Personal to author; cannot be transferred

Duration of Copyright
Type of WorkDuration
Literary, dramatic, musical, artistic (India)Lifetime + 60 years
Joint authorship (India)60 years from death of last surviving author

Dates in a copyright notice (e.g., © 1998, 2003) are dates of creation/modification, NOT expiry dates.

Exceptions — Fair Use (Section 52)
  • Reproduction for private use, research, review
  • Reporting in newspapers, magazines, periodicals
  • Reproduction for judicial proceedings
  • Reproduction by Legislature Secretariats for members' use
  • Performance in educational institutions (staff, students, parents)
  • Making a certified copy as required by law

Punishment for infringement: Min 6 months to 3 years + fine ₹50,000–₹2 lakh. For software piracy: 7 days–3 years + fine (reduced if no pecuniary gain).

Copyright Transfer — Assignment vs. Licensing
Assignment
  • Full transfer of ownership and all rights
  • Must be in writing (signed document)
  • Can be complete or partial
  • Duration can be specified
  • Original owner loses the transferred rights
Licensing
  • Permission to use only; owner retains title
  • Can be exclusive or non-exclusive
  • Limited in scope, territory, and time
  • Royalties paid to the owner
  • Owner can grant multiple licenses
Related Rights (Neighbouring Rights)

Belong to performers, phonogram producers, and broadcasting organisations — the intermediaries who help communicate works to the public. Governed by the Rome Convention (1961), administered jointly by UNESCO, ILO, and WIPO.

Performer's Rights (Section 38)

Includes actors, singers, musicians, dancers, acrobats, jugglers, snake charmers, lecturers.

  • Make/reproduce sound or visual recording
  • Broadcast the performance
  • Communicate to the public other than by broadcast
Broadcaster's Rights (Section 37)

Broadcasting Reproduction Rights — introduced by 1994 amendment.

  • Reproduce the broadcast
  • Cause broadcast to be heard/seen publicly for charges
  • Make sound/visual recordings
  • Reproduce such recordings
  • Sell or hire recordings

Exceptions (Section 39): Private use · Bona fide teaching or research · Reporting current events (excerpts only) · General exceptions under Section 52

Software & Copyright
  • Computer programmes protected as literary works under Berne Convention Article 2(1)
  • Both source code and object code are protected
  • Storing a programme in computer memory = reproduction = covered by copyright
  • "Decompilation" = reproduction + adaptation into analysable form
  • Author = person who conceived the product and directed its creation — not merely the programmer/technician

Information Technology Act, 2000

India became the 12th country to enact cyber law. Based on UNCITRAL's 1996 Model Law on E-Commerce. Came into force 17 October 2000. Provides "functional equivalence" — amends Indian Evidence Act 1872 and Indian Penal Code.

Key Objectives
  • Legal recognition to e-commerce & digital transactions
  • Legal validity of digital signatures
  • Electronic filing with government
  • Electronic storage of data
  • Legal sanction for electronic fund transfers
  • Banks may keep accounts electronically
NOT Applicable To (Section 1[4])
  • Negotiable instruments (except cheques)
  • Power of Attorney
  • Creation of Trust
  • Wills & testamentary dispositions
  • Sale/conveyance of immovable property
  • Other documents notified by Central Government

Scope: Extends to the whole of India. Also applies to offences committed outside India involving a computer/network located in India — regardless of offender's nationality.

Intermediary Liability

An intermediary (Section 2[w]) = any person who receives, stores, or transmits electronic records on behalf of another — includes ISPs, web hosts, search engines, online marketplaces, payment sites, cyber cafes.

Protected from liability if:

  • Has a written policy compliant with Intermediary Guidelines
  • Has appointed a Grievance Officer
  • Acknowledges grievance notice within 36 hours
  • Acts fully within 21 days of notice
  • Takes down infringing content upon valid notification

Intermediary Liability covers: Copyright violations (music, films, images) · Prohibited content (hate speech, racism, pornography)

IT Amendment Act, 2008
  • Section 66: Dishonest/fraudulent acts (destroying, altering, stealing data or source code) — up to 3 years or fine up to ₹5 lakh, or both
  • Section 66A: Sending offensive messages through communication services — up to 3 years + fine

IT Act overall maximum: Imprisonment up to 10 years and compensation up to ₹1 crore.

Privacy vs. Confidentiality vs. Security
Privacy

Individual's right to keep personal data to themselves. Protects consumer information from dissemination or sale without consent.

Confidentiality

Applies to service providers trusted with personal data. Agreements requiring safeguarding data shared by clients.

Security

Protects enterprise/government systems from external threats. Security may not automatically ensure privacy.

Section A — Industrial Design Case Studies
CASE 01 · INDUSTRIAL DESIGN · TECHNOLOGY
Apple iPhone Design
Design PatentsLandmark
Background

Apple introduced the original iPhone in 2007, representing a radical departure from existing mobile phone designs. The design, largely credited to Jony Ive, featured a minimalist form factor, smooth rounded edges, a large glass touchscreen as the entire face, and an absence of physical keyboard or buttons.

Design Features Protected
  • Minimalist rectangular slab with no protruding elements
  • Smooth rounded corners — the specific radius and curvature
  • Proportion of screen to bezel — large front touchscreen
  • Grid icon layout — arrangement of app icons on the home screen
  • Minimalist packaging — white box with product image
Key Legal Battle — Apple v. Samsung

Apple sued Samsung in multiple countries claiming Galaxy phones copied the iPhone's rounded rectangle shape, grid of icons, and other design elements. US litigation lasted years and resulted in damages awards exceeding $1 billion (later reduced). This established that design patents for consumer electronics carry enormous commercial value and are worth vigorously defending globally.

Lesson for IP Law

A product's aesthetic identity can be as commercially valuable as its technical innovations. Design registration is a strategic business tool. Design patents can be enforced simultaneously across multiple jurisdictions worldwide.

CASE 02 · INDUSTRIAL DESIGN · PRODUCT DESIGN
Alessi Juicy Salif Citrus Juicer
Iconic DesignMoMA Collection
Background

Designed by Philippe Starck for Italian design company Alessi in 1990, legend has it Starck sketched the design on a restaurant napkin while eating squid. It became one of the most recognised objects in the history of product design.

Design Features
  • Sculptural, organic form — resembles a space-age insect or squid with three splayed legs
  • Three tapered legs — support the juicer above a glass
  • Pointed conical top — juice-extracting cone, approximately 29 cm tall
  • Cast aluminium — later versions in gold-plated aluminium
Controversy & Cultural Impact

The Juicy Salif is controversial because it prioritises aesthetics over function. Critics note juice often runs down the legs rather than into the glass, seeds aren't filtered, and aluminium reacts with citric acid. Starck himself said it was not meant to squeeze lemons but to start conversations.

IP & Design Significance

Part of MoMA New York's permanent collection. Illustrates a core IP principle: design protection covers aesthetic aspects regardless of functional efficiency. A design need not work optimally to deserve protection — only to be original and aesthetically distinctive. Industrial design can transcend utility and become cultural heritage.

Lesson for IP Law

Industrial design protection is granted for visual/aesthetic character independent of functional performance. This is why the legal definition explicitly excludes "any mode or principle of construction."

CASE 03 · INDUSTRIAL DESIGN · TRANSPORTATION
Vespa Scooter — Piaggio (1946)
Heritage DesignCultural Icon
Background

Designed by aeronautical engineer Corradino D'Ascanio for Piaggio in post-war Italy (1946). D'Ascanio disliked conventional motorcycles and brought an aircraft designer's eye — resulting in an entirely novel form of personal transport.

Design Features
  • Monocoque steel body — single-piece frame forming both structure and aesthetic shell (unlike conventional motorcycles)
  • Enclosed engine — integrated within body, protecting rider's clothes — revolutionary at the time
  • Streamlined organic curves — drawn from aeronautical design principles
  • Distinctive front fairing — curved front shield wrapping around handlebars
  • Step-through frame — allowed riders in formal wear or skirts to mount comfortably
Cultural Impact

Became a global symbol of Italian style, famously appearing in the 1953 film Roman Holiday with Audrey Hepburn and Gregory Peck. Over 19 million Vespas sold worldwide.

IP & Design Significance

Demonstrates design longevity — a well-protected industrial design can maintain commercial and cultural relevance across 75+ years. Piaggio has consistently registered design updates while preserving the iconic visual identity. Shows how design protection can coexist and reinforce trademark protection for overall brand identity.

CASE 04 · INDUSTRIAL DESIGN · WATCHMAKING
Swatch Watch — Swatch Group (1983)
Design InnovationIndustry Revival
Background

Introduced in 1983 as a response to the Swiss watchmaking crisis — the industry was losing market share to cheaper Japanese quartz watches. Led by Ernst Thomke and designed by Jacques Müller and Elmar Mock, Swatch reimagined what a watch could be.

Design Features
  • Colourful injection-moulded plastic casing — breaking the convention of metal or leather watches
  • Minimalist, bold dial design with strong visual identity
  • Only 51 components (vs. 90+ in conventional Swiss watches) — enabled thinner profile and lower cost
  • Ultrasonic welding — permanently sealed casing; designed for replacement not repair
  • Seasonal design collections — like fashion collections, creating collector demand
Commercial Impact

Originally priced at CHF 50, positioned as a fashion accessory. Special editions produced with artists Keith Haring and Kiki Picasso, turning them into collectibles. The strategy revived the Swiss watch industry.

IP & Design Significance

Demonstrates how industrial design can democratise access while creating genuine IP value. Each seasonal design collection required new registrations. Shows that design used as the primary differentiator (rather than technical function) can save entire industries. Also illustrates interaction between design, fashion, and art in creating additional IP layers.

CASE 05 · INDUSTRIAL DESIGN · CONSUMER GOODS
Oxo Good Grips Kitchen Tools
Universal DesignUser-Centred
Background

Developed in 1990 by Sam Farber for Oxo International after he noticed his wife Betsey, who had mild arthritis, struggled with conventional kitchen tool handles. Farber hired Smart Design LLC to pioneer a new approach.

Design Features
  • Oversized oval handles — large enough for comfortable grip; oval cross-section prevents rolling
  • Soft non-slip Santoprene rubber — distinctive black rubber fins that flex and conform to the hand
  • Fin design — raised fins improve grip and signal ergonomic intent visually
  • Ambidextrous use — intuitive controls for all hand orientations
  • Durable materials — stainless steel combined with soft-grip bodies
Universal Design Philosophy

Oxo pioneered Universal Design (Inclusive Design) — designing for people with the widest range of physical abilities. Products designed for people with arthritis or limited grip are paradoxically more comfortable for everyone. "Design for the extremes" produces mainstream products with broader appeal.

IP & Design Significance

Demonstrates that design protection is valuable not just for luxury goods but for everyday utilitarian products. The distinctive soft-grip handle was widely imitated, making design protection essential. Proves that functional aesthetics and social inclusivity can be commercially successful — and legally protectable — design strategies.

Section B — Indian Copyright & Broadcasting Cases
06

Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. v. Entertainment Network (India) Ltd.

COPYRIGHT BOARD · BROADCASTING RIGHTS · COMPULSORY LICENCE UNDER SECTION 31

Appellant
Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. (T-Series)
v.
Respondent
Entertainment Network India Ltd. (Radio Mirchi FM)
Background & Facts

T-Series owned copyrights in a large catalogue of musical works. Entertainment Network India Ltd. (ENIL), operating Radio Mirchi FM, had been broadcasting T-Series songs without any licence or authorization. In legal proceedings, ENIL admitted this infringement. ENIL then applied to the Copyright Board for a compulsory licence under Section 31 to broadcast T-Series songs. The Copyright Board directed the Registrar to grant the licence. T-Series challenged this, arguing it rewarded wrongdoing.

Issues
  • Can a compulsory licence be granted to an admitted infringer?
  • Is the Copyright Board's power to grant licences mandatory or discretionary?
  • How to balance private copyright rights vs. public interest?
Decision / Held

(i) Granting a compulsory licence to an admitted infringer would put a premium on default and encourage others to first infringe and then seek a licence when caught. This was held impermissible.

(ii) Section 31(1) uses the word "may" — the Copyright Board has discretion, not a mandatory obligation to grant licences. Licence can be granted or refused on valid grounds after sufficient inquiry.

(iii) The Board must maintain a delicate balance between the private rights of the copyright owner and larger public interest.

Significance

Establishes that the compulsory licence mechanism cannot be weaponized by infringers to legitimize wrongdoing. Reinforces voluntary licensing as the primary mechanism. Clarifies discretionary nature of Copyright Board powers and the public interest–private rights balance.

07

Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. v. Bathla Cassette Industries Pvt. Ltd.

DELHI HIGH COURT · VERSION RECORDINGS · SECTION 52(1)(j) · PERFORMER'S RIGHTS

Plaintiff
Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. (T-Series)
v.
Defendant
Bathla Cassette Industries Pvt. Ltd.
Background & Facts

T-Series produced a version recording (cover version) of "Chalo Dildar Chalo" from the film Pakeezah (originally owned by Mahal Pictures) under Section 52(1)(j) — paying ₹400 royalty for 10,000 copies to Mahal Pictures. The cover was released on cassettes titled "Yadein Vol. I & II" sung by lesser-known singers under titles suggesting they were sung by Mukesh, Lata, and Rafi.

When Bathla attempted to produce a cover of T-Series' version — following the same s.52(1)(j) procedure and offering T-Series a royalty — T-Series sought an injunction, claiming independent copyright in their version recording.

Core Legal Question

Can a version recording made under s.52(1)(j) itself claim independent copyright protection? Can T-Series — having benefited from s.52 to cover someone else's song — prevent others from doing the same to their cover?

T-Series Argued
  • Engaged musicians, singers, conductors independently
  • Used independent skill and labour
  • Created a "substantially new arrangement"
  • Therefore entitled to separate copyright
Bathla Argued
  • s.52 is a statutory defence, not a creative original work
  • Section 13(3)(b): no copyright in a sound recording made infringing another copyright
  • 1994 amendment: performer's rights of original artists violated by substituting them
  • T-Series cannot prevent what it itself did
Decision / Held

T-Series' claim for injunction was not sustainable. A work created under s.52(1)(j) statutory defence cannot claim independent copyright — the section pre-supposes that the derived product would otherwise have been an infringement. Having sought s.52 protection, T-Series acknowledged their work was a copy, not an original. The defendant was entitled to do to T-Series' version exactly what T-Series had done to Mahal Pictures' original.

Significance

Landmark case on limits of statutory licence for cover versions. A party cannot simultaneously claim the benefit of a statutory exception and then claim full copyright for that work. Also highlights how the 1994 amendment's performer's rights protect original artists — substituting them with lesser singers may constitute a violation.

08

Video Master v. Nishi Productions

COPYRIGHT · DIVISIBILITY OF RIGHTS · CABLE TV vs. SATELLITE BROADCASTING

Plaintiff
Video Master (video copyright holder)
v.
Defendant
Nishi Productions (film producer)
Background & Facts

Video Master claimed it was assigned sole and exclusive video copyrights in the film "Bees Saal Baad" by Nishi Productions. The dispute arose when Nishi entered into a separate agreement with another distributor who used the film to make cassettes for satellite TV transmission. Video Master alleged violation of their exclusive video copyright.

Issue

Does an assignment of "video copyright" include cable TV and satellite broadcasting rights? Are these the same right or distinct rights?

Decision / Held

(i) Cable TV rights and satellite broadcasting rights are two distinct and different rights under the Copyright Act. They can exist in different persons simultaneously without either infringing the other.

(ii) Receiving satellite signals on a dish antenna and relaying them through cable to viewers does not constitute a violation of the plaintiff's exclusive video copyright. These are separate modes of exploitation requiring separate assignments.

Significance

Fundamental case on the divisibility of copyright. Copyright is a bundle of distinct rights — each mode of exploitation (video, cable, satellite, theatrical) can be separately assigned to different parties. When drafting copyright assignments, every mode of exploitation must be specifically enumerated. Silence is construed narrowly.

09

Garware Plastics and Polysters Ltd. v. Telelink

BOMBAY HIGH COURT · CABLE TV AS PUBLIC BROADCAST · DEFINING "PUBLIC"

Plaintiff
Garware Plastics and Polysters Ltd.
v.
Defendant
Telelink (cable operators)
Background & Facts

Garware owned copyright in films and had assigned the right to broadcast via Doordarshan to the Government of India, but retained the right to telecast via cable television. Telelink — a cable operator — was showing Garware's films to subscribers without permission. The key question: is showing a film on cable a broadcast to the "public", thereby infringing copyright?

Issues
  • Does cable TV transmission constitute a "broadcast" to the "public"?
  • Do cable TV subscribers constitute "the public" or a private group?
Decision / Held

(i) Whether a communication is to the public depends on the nature of the receiving persons. If they can be characterised as "the public or a portion of the public," the communication is a broadcast. The court held cable TV transmission is a broadcast to the public.

(ii) Cable TV viewers are not members of one family or their guests — they have no homogeneity. They are spread across various apartments and public places in a locality. Therefore they are members of the public.

(iii) The defendants were violating the plaintiff's copyright by broadcasting films via cable without the rights holder's consent.

Significance

Defined the crucial boundary between private communication and public broadcast in the cable TV context. The "family/homogeneous group" test distinguishes private from public communication. Established that cable operators need separate licences from copyright holders. Contributed to the regulatory framework bringing cable operators under licensing obligations.

Section C — Cyber Law Cases
10

Yahoo! Inc. v. Akash Arora & Anr.

DELHI HIGH COURT · 78 (1999) DLT 285 · CYBERSQUATTING · DOMAIN NAMES & TRADEMARKS

Plaintiff
Yahoo! Inc. (USA)
v.
Defendant
Akash Arora & Anr.
Background & Facts

Yahoo! Inc. operates the globally famous portal yahoo.com. Akash Arora registered and operated www.yahooindia.com — a website with virtually identical branding, look, feel, and content to Yahoo!'s official site, targeting Indian internet users. Yahoo! filed suit for trademark infringement, passing off, and copyright infringement. At the time, the Indian Trade Marks Act, 1999 had no explicit provision protecting domain names. The defendant argued domain names were merely technical addresses, not trademarks.

Issues
  • Are domain names entitled to trademark protection under Indian law without explicit statutory provision?
  • Does "yahooindia.com" infringe Yahoo!'s trademark?
  • Does similarity in look, feel, and content constitute copyright violation?
Decision / Held — Delhi High Court

(i) First Indian case to directly link trademarks and domain names. Domain names are entitled to trademark protection under Indian law even without explicit statutory provision. "Yahoo!" is a well-known trademark with extensive goodwill.

(ii) "yahooindia.com" is deceptively similar and constitutes passing off and trademark infringement — likely to cause confusion to internet users.

(iii) Similarities in look and feel and content of the website constitute copyright violations in addition to trademark violation. Injunction granted against the defendant.

Significance

India's first major cybersquatting case. Established: domain names are protectable as trademarks · internet users can be confused by domain names just as by product names · website design can attract copyright protection. Supreme Court reiterated this in Satyam Infoway v. Sifynet (2004) 6 SCC 145. Led to development of India's INDRP (domain dispute resolution) modelled on ICANN's UDRP.

11

T-Series v. MySpace (Delhi High Court)

DELHI HIGH COURT · INTERMEDIARY LIABILITY · ONLINE COPYRIGHT INFRINGEMENT · SAFE HARBOUR

Plaintiff
Super Cassettes Industries Ltd. (T-Series)
v.
Defendant
MySpace Inc. (social networking / video platform)
Background & Facts

T-Series sued MySpace — the social networking platform — for copyright infringement after users uploaded T-Series' copyrighted songs and music videos without authorization. T-Series alleged MySpace facilitated and profited from this infringement without taking adequate steps to prevent it. MySpace claimed the "safe harbour" protection as a mere intermediary hosting user-uploaded content.

Central Legal Question

Under what circumstances is an online platform liable for users' copyright infringement? What obligations must an intermediary fulfil to claim safe harbour protection?

Decision / Key Principles

(i) An intermediary cannot claim blanket immunity. Safe harbour requires implementing systems to remove infringing content upon notice from rights holders.

(ii) Where the platform has actual knowledge of infringement (through rights holder notices) and fails to act, it loses safe harbour and becomes liable.

(iii) Distinction between proactive monitoring (cannot be mandated) and responsive takedowns (can and must be done upon notice). Platforms cannot pre-screen all content but must act swiftly on specific notice.

(iv) Court directed MySpace to implement a metadata-based filtering system to help identify and block infringing content in future.

Significance

Foundational to intermediary liability in the Indian internet context. Established the "notice and takedown" regime as the baseline. Clarified that safe harbour is conditional, not absolute — platforms must have robust grievance mechanisms. Distinguished active from passive participation in infringement. Influenced framing of intermediary liability rules in the IT Amendment Act 2008 and subsequent Intermediary Guidelines.

Common Exam Confusions — Cleared
⚠️

Design vs. Copyright: Industrial design applies when a design is applied to a mass-produced article and judged solely by the eye. If it cannot be applied to an article (e.g., a painting), it falls under copyright as an artistic work. Some designs also qualify as trademarks. The categories are not mutually exclusive.

⚠️

Assignment vs. License: Assignment = transfer of ownership itself. License = you retain ownership but give permission. An assignee becomes the new owner; a licensee only gets permission within agreed terms. Both must be in writing for copyright.

⚠️

Digital Signature vs. Electronic Signature: All digital signatures are electronic signatures (it's a subset), but not vice versa. Digital signatures use PKI, asymmetric cryptography, and certificate authority validation. Electronic signatures is the broader category including scanned signatures, ticks, etc.

⚠️

Broadcaster's right ≠ Author's copyright: They are separate, stackable rights. The broadcaster's right does not merge with or extinguish the original copyright in the broadcast content. Even for public-domain works, broadcasters retain separate rights. Broadcaster's right is based on investment, not creative contribution.

⚠️

Copyright is automatic: It begins at creation and fixation in tangible form. Registration is NOT a prerequisite for protection in India or under the Berne Convention. Registration helps enforce rights in court (especially US), but the right itself exists from the moment of creation.

⚠️

Ideas are NOT protected: Copyright protects the expression of ideas, not the ideas themselves. Two authors can independently write about the same idea — each expression is protected, but neither can monopolise the underlying concept, method, procedure, or mathematical formula. (TRIPS Article 9.2).

⚠️

IT Act applicability: Extends to offences outside India if a computer/network in India is involved — regardless of offender's nationality. Does NOT apply to wills, negotiable instruments (except cheques), trusts, powers of attorney, or sale of immovable property.
